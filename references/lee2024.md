# Why Do Animals Need Shaping? A Theory of Task Composition and Curriculum Learning

*Authors:* Jin Hwa Lee, Stefano Sarao Mannelli, Andrew Saxe
*Link:* https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18361

## Summary

Animals (and, empirically, artificial agents) often learn complex behaviors faster when
first trained on simpler component tasks ("shaping"), but prior to this work there was
no rigorous theory explaining why shaping helps or how to design it. The authors build a
theoretical framework, using statistical-physics tools for analyzing deep policy-gradient
learning, that models how a multilayer policy network learns task primitives under
different training protocols, including pre-training on individual primitives before
combining them into a composite task.

The analysis characterizes a complex interplay between task complexity and the
effectiveness of shaping: structured/staged training protocols can expose useful task
primitives inside the network, yielding faster and more robust learning than training
directly on the composite task, and the theory gives quantitative predictions for when
shaping helps and how to design an effective curriculum.

For the citing problem, this paper (alongside patel2023, from overlapping authors) is
cited as one of the "viable models" — a statistical-mechanics style toy model of deep
policy learning — that could be adapted to formalize how persona/latent selection
develops over the course of training, including curriculum-like effects during RLHF.
